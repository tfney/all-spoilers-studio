Title: One Dollar - Black characters as plot devices
---

Characters:
- Jake Noveer - Brilliant, but jaded detective. Managing a tense relationship with his best friend Trask, Braden police chief, due to his perception that Trask abandoned the missing persons case of a young black girl.
- Tony "Rook" (?) 
- Dante
- Preacher (Etta?)*
- Foster mom
- Markell
- other mill worker* (Dante looking for job) only person who seemed to want to go out of his way, fellow black man
- Rand
- Waitress*
- Cass*
*starred characters are those who were no consistently a part of the storyline and/or didn't contribute substantially to the plot

Character Placement:
Black characters are always the only black character unless they are interacting with each other.

Rand is the only black person with the "elites" and he is a scammer.

Dannie's family loses everything which means she needs to get a job instead of go to college. She goes to the "other side" because she never learned to do anything "fundamental". Jake had earlier referenced that Cass opened her shop in a less promising part of town. It seems strange that it was written that Dannie had to go to the part of town where it seems only the black characters exist so she could learn some basic skills. Were there no bakeries in Dannie's part of town? Why not just have her continue to babysit for Garrett? He came into some money, they seemed to care for each other, so wouldn't it be more natural to continue that arrangement?

Also, why not have Cass open her shop in a better part of town? She seemed to at least have the option, but then they couldn't have connected her to Jake so easily.

*its not unreasonable to think there's a much higher proportion of white people to black people in the kind of city Braden is modeled after but I'm struggling to think that the result is such a lack of integration, especially in such a small city.
	- Follow up on this point: I checked for examples of real cities Braden is modeled after. One was 54% white, 35% black, another was 49% white, 36% black, and the 3rd was 8% white and 72% black. I know these are only 3 examples but taking it for what it is, it seems like Braden does not represent reality. I know it's fiction but I think it furthers my point because it looks like there should have been more black characters on the whole. So it feels like the strategic placement of the very few that actually exist was strictly to create a racial plot line. 
	- For consideration: Gabby Solis is the only hispanic main character amongst an almost completely white cast but her character is never used as a plot device (from a racial perspective) [[Desperate White]]
	

Dynamics:
- Noveer takes interest in Rook for two reasons: (1) He recognizes an opportunity to use her to embed himself in the investigation and (2) They have the shared experience of being the only black cops in the department. The second point seemed to be the real point here because Jake seemed totally capable of manipulating situations however he needed to to get what he wanted. I think the primary goal here, again, was to force the plot to put two black characters together. 
- Dante is a part of Bud's crew but the only black man. He also happens to be the only one who doesn't reap the full benefit of being a part of Bud's crew. Bud seemed to look out for him only to the extent that it wasn't to the detriment of his other (white) guys. 
	- reference Jake's "tribal" comment to Trask.
- Chewy is openly racist to Rook. It's written in a way that I think that we're supposed to understand that he doesn't mean any real harm and that he in unconscious of his own racism, but it doesn't change the fact. His statements are blatant and apparently tolerated by everybody. ]
	- It's interesting that Jake who is very vocal about Trask's apparent racism (which I don't think is obvious to anyone, and probably not factual at all) and the racism in general, somehow finds a way to rationalize Chewy's behavior (who is clearly racist)
		- Reference Jake's comments to Rook about Chewy being a jerk, but...
- Dante's episode is the only one with no actual "resolution" in the end (need to double check on this). Nobody came out of their situations perfectly clean and fixed, but I think it's laughable that Dante, who was having some very real problems (in that he seemed to be in a more precarious financial situation than the others and maybe mental health challenges as well) was suddenly released from his woes by one attendance at church (which wasn't even really church, it was some random person's funeral).
- Rook was put right into the center of a storyline involving a white supremacist group, which was wholly unnecessary. Then, more specifically, she is used as a tool to incense the wife of the guy she shot. They wrote it to sound like the wife would be upset she was there because she shot him, but that seems secondary to the fact that she is upset because Rook is black.
